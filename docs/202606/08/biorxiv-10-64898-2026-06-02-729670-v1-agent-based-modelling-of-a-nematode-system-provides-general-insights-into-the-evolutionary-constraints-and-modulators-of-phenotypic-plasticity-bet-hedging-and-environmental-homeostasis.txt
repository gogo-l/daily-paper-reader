# Abstract 26 

In this study, I implemented an agent -based model aimed at exploring the competition between 27 

plastic and non -plastic genotypes in a digital environment with periodic fluctuations in food 28 

resources , using the dimorphic nematode  Pristionchus pacificus  as a proxy for mixed -strategy 29 

systems showing a combination of stochastic and conditional phenotype production . Emerging 30 

behaviour s generated  in response to variation in three  main  variables, that is, i) intrinsic cost of 31 

plasticity, ii) timescale of environmental fluctuation, and iii) degree of plasticity,  were  monitored in 32 

terms of frequency  and time  to fixation of two alleles of the developmental switch gene  eud -1, one 33 

enabling mouth -form dimorphism and predation, the other leading to the constitutive expression of 34 

a single , bacterivorous  morph.  Interestingly, while intermediate -to -long  periods of environmental 35 

stability (in terms of generations) and a higher level of plasticity might favour the evolution of plastic 36 

strategies in a “cost -free” condition, the introduction and increase of inherent costs of plasticity 37 

could make pure bet -hedging more advantageous, induce a sequential collapse in  the  frequency of 38 

fixation of plastic strains and time of coexistence between strains, and make the invasion by non -39 

plastic mutants more likely until a plateau is reached. In addition, asymmetries in fitness between 40 

the two morphs might  be an almost necessary condition to  enable the invasion of a non -plastic 41 

population by plastic genotype s. Collectively,  while  confirming  some  previous theoretical findings ,42

# 1.  Introducti on 50 

The relationship  between genotype and phenotype  is one of the cornerstones of biology , and the 51 

environment  often plays  a pivotal  role  in directing the process of conversion of genetic information 52 

into traits  at every  phenotypic level , from proteins up to  complex  behaviour s. Indeed, traits may 53 

either  remain stable  or be expressed differently  in response to environmental  stimuli . The former 54 

property is known as  environmental homeostasis  (Pigliucci, 2010) , while the latter is  called 55 

phenotypic plasticity , that is commonly  defined as  the ability of a genotype to produce different 56 

phenotypes in response to different environmental conditions  and  is  an important , astonishingly 57 

widespread  strategy  which may  enabl e survival in rapidly changing e cological contexts  or allow the 58 

colonisation of new niches  (Pigliucci, 2001; Ghalambor et al., 2007; Fusco & Minelli, 2010; Bonamour 59 

et al., 2019) . The pervasiveness of  plasticity  both among prokaryotes and eukaryotes  is clear when 60 

looking at  the impressive number of case studies  reported  in the literature and referring to a  broad 61 

variety of taxa , from single -celled organisms  to trees or mammals . Remarkable examples  of plasticity 62 

include  regulation of molecule production in bacteria  in response to cell density  (Kummerli et al., 63 

2009) , repeated modifications of body length in marine iguanas  depending on food availability 64

the temperature experienced during embryogenesis  (Vedel et al., 2008) , and  countless  others 66 

involving the most diverse  traits, either with continuous or discrete variation ( polyphenism ), 67 

triggering factors,  and potential for reversibility  of the response  (Bento et al., 2010; Lin et al., 2018; 68 

Escobar-Sandoval et al., 2021; Mu et al., 2021; Sjulgård et al., 2021; Fogg et al., 2022; Marian et al., 69

As a matter of fact , metabolic, physiological, and developmental processes are often context -71 

dependent  and intrinsically  sensitive to variables including temperature and nutrient availabilit y72 

(Nijhout, 2002) , but  it  is  largely recognised  that plasticity  may  also have a genetic basis  and  be  the 73 

result of  transcriptional, post -transcriptional, or epigenetic regulations , which make s it  evolvable 74 

(Chevin et al., 2013; Peltier et al., 2018; Fortuna, 2022; Kovuri et al., 2023) . For instance, evidence 75 

that even single -nucleotide mutations can  affect  the  degree of plasticity  of  phenotypic  traits , that is, 76 

the  magnitude of the plastic response  along an environmental gradient , wa s found in yeasts  (Duveau 77 

et al., 2017)  and  nematodes  (Vigne et al., 2021) , and natural selection may explain  inter -generational 78 

changes in th is degree of sensitivity  both in the laboratory  (Walworth et al., 2016; Mallard et al., 79 

2020)  and in the wild  (Nussey et al., 2005) . The latter case studies  clearly  demonstrate that 80 

evolutionary processes can  indeed  modulate  plasticity , leading to  an increase , maintenance, or 81 

reduction  of its level , including the  complete loss of this ability .82 

Th ese dynamics  may  be governed by  the impact of trait plasticity on  fitness , that is,  the success of 83 

an organism in terms of offspring produced and genetic material passed on to the next generation .84 

More specifically, p lasticity  may be  maladaptive, neutral, or adaptive  (Ghalambor et al., 2007) ,85 

depending on whether it has negative, null, or positive effects on fitness, respectively . For instance, 86 

it may be favoured by natural selection  when  organisms face  heterogeneous  or variable 87 

environments, assuming that  i)  the plastic response move s the mean phenotype  closer to the 88

the  cost of plasticity  is  low . Such fitness cost  might be related to additional regulatory genetic 90 

machinery,  although  it is  usually negligible and/or hard to detect  (Murren et al., 2015; Hendry, 2015) .91 

In these conditions, plasticity can be considered a  generalist strategy  (Chevin et al., 2013) . In 92 

contrast , environments  characterised by  infrequent variation or unpredictable patterns of variation 93 

select for  fixed , unconditionally expressed  traits . When expressing  a genetically determined trait 94 

confers  upon an individual  the highest fitness in a specific environment,  the  considered  individual is 95 

adopting a  specialist strategy  (Acasuso -Rivero et al., 2019) . Indeed , a reduction  or loss of plasticity 96 

may be beneficial when plasticity is maladaptive,  that is , when  the response  induced by the 97 

environment move s the mean phenotype further from the optimum  (Storz & Scott, 2021) . Finally, 98 

plasticity may have no effects on fitness,  which implies that  its evolution is likely to depend on 99 

stochastic mechanisms such as genetic drift  (Gomulkiewicz & Stinchcombe, 2022) .100 

Environment -dependent plasticity can also evolve in combination with bet -hedging, that is another 101 

key evolutionary solution observed in unpredictably changing environments and represents a risk -102 

spreading strategy that leads to a reduction in mean fitness while offering a beneficial trade -off 103 

across generations by reducing fitness variance  (Yasui & Garcia -Gonzalez, 2016; Susoy & Sommer, 104 

2016) . An example of this mixed strategy, also known as coin -flipping plasticity, was reported in pea 105 

aphids  (Grantham et al., 2016) . Indeed, although the probability to produce winged  or wingless 106 

offspring in this species depends on the level of crowding experienced by asexual  females , both 107 

phenotypes  are produced by individual aphids. It is acknowledged that this balance between 108 

developmental sensitivity and stochasticity may be promoted by a combination of partial reliability 109 

of environmental cues and between -generations environmental variation  (Donaldson -Matasci et al., 110 

2013) .111

Along with research  using  experimental evolution  (reviewed in  Kassen  (2002) ) and analytical 112 

treatments  (Gabriel & Lynch, 1992; van Tienderen, 1997; King & Hadfield, 2019; Kasada & Yoshida, 113 

2020) , computational modelling  and simulation s constitute  an  important  approach  that has shed 114 

light on  the conditions driving the  competition between plastic and non -plastic genotypes . For 115 

instance , the potential of digital organisms for testing theoretical predictions  including the 116 

hypothesis  that variable and static environments select for and against plasticity, respectively, has 117 

recently been highlighted  (Fortuna, 2022) . Using the Avida Digital Evolution Platform,  Lalejini and 118 

Ofria  (2016)  investigat ed the selective pressures leading to plasticity  and , in addition to  obtain ing 119 

outcomes consistent with previous assessments  including Ghalambor  et al.  (2010) , they also 120 

observed  that traits  we re generally expressed unconditionally  prior to the evolution of conditional 121 

trait expression and reported the evolution of sub -optimal forms of plasticity before optimal forms. 122 

Other  insights  of general interest  can be found  in  another study involving digital plastic and non -123 

plastic organisms facing different environmental conditions  (Miras, 2024) , i.e. , that  genetic costs of 124 

plasticity might undermine  the  potential benefits  of this strategy . A similar approach is also found in 125 

research  using  agent -based modelling , e.g.,  Edelaar et al.  (2017) , where the authors explored  the 126 

conditions  leading to  either adaptive plasticity or matching habitat choice as possible solutions to 127 

environmental heterogeneity  with  the  simulation  platform NetLogo , and Kalirad & Sommer  (2024) ,128 

that embraced an integrative theoretical -experimental framework by  combining  laboratory 129 

measurements  of the magnitude of phenotypic plasticity  across two diets  in the exemplary 130 

nematode species  Pristionchus pacificus  with the aim of  investigating the effects of plasticity  and 131 

stochastic phenotype production  on competition and coexistence of different strains. 132 

Agent -based modelling  is a  technique  used to implement  computational  tools  (i.e.,  agent -based 133 

models , hereafter referred to as ABMs ) through which the global behaviour of a  complex  system can 134 

be simulated as a product of multiple interactions among independent individuals called  agents . In 135

2.2 Model overview 192 

The purpose of the model is to simulate the competition between plastic and non -plastic genotypes 193 

(depending on the state of the developmental switch , X -linked  gene  eud -1 (Ragsdale et al., 2013) ) of 194 

the dimorphic nematode worm  Pristionchus pacificus  in an in vitro  environment  with periodic 195 

dietary  fluctuations.  The model  allows investigation  of the  effects of variables including  an intrinsic 196 

cost of plasticity, the timescale of environmental fluctuations  in terms of non -overlapping 197 

generations , and the individual degree of plasticity on the evolutionary trajectories of populations 198

2. 3 Simulated scenarios 264 

Since agent -based models rely on large numbers of computational individuals whose interactions 265 

are  often  regulated by probabilistic instructions, the final outcomes resulting from a single run are 266 

expected to differ from those generated by another run  shar ing the same settings  (Hunter & 267 

Kelleher, 2020) . For this reason, all results  produced by  each  simulated  scenario are extracted from 268 

multiple  runs, more specifically, 100 runs for Symmetric Analyses (SAs) and 1000 runs for Invasibility 269 

Analyses (IAs). Symmetric Analyses start with an equal number of  P. pacificus  nematodes belonging 270 

to two alternative strains (i.e., plastic vs non -plastic), whereas Invasibility Analyses start with a single 271 

mutant or immigrant individual belonging to a strain  different  from that of the population to be 272 

invaded. 273 

While micro -validation of  PhePlastiComp  at the agent level is based on extensive comparisons 274 

between characteristics /rules assigned to  the  computational individuals and empirical data (see 275 

Supplementary Files 1 and 2), s ome basic, preliminary scenarios are used to validate  qualitatively 276 

the model at a system level  (macro -validation) , based on the match between the  outcomes of runs 277 

and some  expected  patterns  that can be inferred from the relevant literature  (see Table  3A).  The 278 

outcomes of these preliminary scenarios are presented in Supplementary File 3. 279

# 4.  Concluding remarks 969 

The  opportunity to model the complex interplay between  environment, development and  genetics, 970 

combined with a focus on  individual heterogeneity , makes agent -based modelling an ideal approach 971 

to  generate theoretical insights using empirical information from  species  that have already proven 972 

to be powerful experimental subjects. 973 

Collectively, the scenarios presented in this article support the suitability of  PhePlastiComp  as a 974 

reliable tool to explore the i mpact of multiple  evolutionary  factors  on the emerging behaviour of the 975 

system , using a digital counterpart of a well -established model organism . Although  the model  can 976 

be used to  investigate competition  between hypothetical plastic and non -plastic strains of  P. 977 

pacificus  depending on the state of the developmental switch  eud -1, I argue that some of  the trends 978 

detected here could also  provide  broader suggestions on the conditions favouring plastic, stochastic ,979 

or constitutive phenotype expression in  other  taxa . Indeed, while parameterisation based on  P. 980
