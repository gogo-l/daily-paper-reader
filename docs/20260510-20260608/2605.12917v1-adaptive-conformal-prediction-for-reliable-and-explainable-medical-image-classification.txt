# Adaptive Conformal Prediction for Reliable and Explainable Medical Image Classification 

One Octadion [0009 −0008 −6823 −2816] , Novanto Yudistira [0000 −0001 −5330 −5930] , and Lailil Muflikhah [0000 −0001 −7903 −0576] 

Faculty of Computer Science, Universitas Brawijaya, Malang, Indonesia 

octadion@student.ub.ac.id, {yudistira,lailil}@ub.ac.id 

Abstract. Deep learning models for medical imaging often exhibit over-confidence, creating safety risks in ambiguous diagnostic scenarios. While Conformal Prediction (CP) provides distribution-free statistical guaran-tees, standard methods like Regularized Adaptive Prediction Sets (RAPS) optimize for average efficiency, masking severe failures on difficult inputs. We propose an Adaptive Lambda Criterion for RAPS that minimizes the worst-case coverage violation across prediction set size strata. On Or-ganAMNIST (58,850 abdominal CT images, 11 classes), standard size-optimized RAPS converges to near-deterministic behavior with stratified undercoverage on uncertain samples, while our method achieves 95.72% global coverage with average set size 1.09 and ≥90% coverage across all strata. Cross-domain validation on PathMNIST (107,180 pathology im-ages, 9 classes) confirms generalizability. Quantitative Grad-CAM anal-ysis ( ρ = −0.30 , p < 10 −22 ) confirms that multi-label predictions corre-spond to focused attention on anatomically ambiguous regions. 

Keywords: Medical Image Classification · Conformal Prediction · Un-certainty Quantification · Stratified Coverage · Explainable AI 

## 1 Introduction 

The integration of Deep Learning (DL) into radiological workflows promises to revolutionize diagnostic efficiency, particularly in automated analysis of abdomi-nal Computed Tomography (CT) scans [1]. However, translation to clinical prac-tice is stalled by a lack of reliable uncertainty quantification. Standard classifiers are frequently overconfident, assigning high probabilities even to ambiguous or misclassified inputs [2]. In high-stakes medical settings, a model that is “mostly correct” but confidently wrong on difficult cases violates the core tenet of safety. Conformal Prediction (CP) has emerged as a rigorous framework to address this, transforming model outputs into prediction sets guaranteed to contain the true label with high probability [3]. Yet, applying standard CP methods to med-ical imaging reveals a dangerous limitation. Our analysis on OrganAMNIST shows that while Regularized Adaptive Prediction Sets (RAPS) [11] meet global coverage targets, they suffer from stratified undercoverage: standard RAPS pro-duces prediction sets of size ≥ 2 for only 10 out of 17,778 test samples, with

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Uncertainty Quantification in Medical AI 

Predictive uncertainty estimation is a fundamental challenge across machine learning [19], with particular urgency in medical applications where prediction Adaptive Conformal Prediction for Medical Image Classification 3

errors carry life-threatening consequences. Deep learning has been widely ap-plied to medical prediction tasks spanning radiology, genomics [20], and disease prognosis [21], yet standard models trained with cross-entropy loss tend to force single predictions even in ambiguous cases, leading to overconfidence [2]. For medical image classification specifically, CNNs like ResNet [5] serve as backbones but struggle with epistemic ambiguity in low-resolution benchmarks [1], particularly bilateral organ symmetry. Bayesian approaches including MC Dropout [7] and Deep Ensembles [8] improve calibration but incur high computational costs or require multiple models. Post-hoc methods like Temperature Scaling [2] im-prove probability reliability but lack individual safety guarantees [3]. 

2.2 Conformal Prediction Methodologies 

Conformal Prediction (CP) provides distribution-free statistical guarantees on prediction sets [6], making it a principled framework for safety-critical appli-cations. For a comprehensive survey, see Zhou et al. [22]. Early classification approaches like LAC [9] used fixed thresholds but failed to adapt to data het-eroscedasticity. Romano et al. [10] introduced Adaptive Prediction Sets (APS), accumulating probability mass until reaching target confidence. To address the “long tail” issue, Angelopoulos et al. [11] proposed RAPS, adding a penalty λ for larger sets—currently the state-of-the-art for multi-class tasks. Seedat et al. [24] further improved adaptive CP using self-supervised learning. Recent applications demonstrate CP’s versatility across domains: genomic medicine [12], graph neural networks [13], semantic segmentation [14], and con-formal triage for clinical deployment [15]. However, most works apply standard CP directly without addressing stratified failures on difficult subgroups. 

2.3 The Gap: Stratified Coverage 

Standard CP coverage guarantees are marginal (averaged over populations), al-lowing global validity while systematically failing on difficult subgroups. Recent works highlight the need for size-stratified [3, 16] and class-conditional cover-age [16]. Gauthier et al. [23] formalize adaptive coverage policies, and Babbar et al. [25] demonstrate the utility of well-calibrated prediction sets in human–AI collaboration. Our work addresses this gap with an Adaptive Lambda criterion ensuring uniform coverage across uncertainty levels, preventing safety compro-mises on difficult diagnostic cases.

3.2 Base Classifier Architecture 

Our base model fθ is a ResNet-18 [5] pretrained on ImageNet-1K. The final fully connected layer is replaced with a Dropout layer ( p=0 .3) followed by a linear projection to RK (where K=11 for OrganAMNIST, K=9 for PathMNIST). The model is trained to minimize Cross-Entropy loss with label smoothing ( ϵ=0 .1)to prevent overfitting and encourage cluster tightness. Optimization uses the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 and cosine annealing schedule. We employ early stopping with patience of 10 epochs based on validation accuracy.

3.4 Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We evaluate on two MedMNIST benchmarks [1]: (1) OrganAM-NIST : 58,850 abdominal CT images across 11 organ classes ( 28 ×28 grayscale, upsampled to 224 ×224 ), split into 34,581/6,491/17,778 for train/val/test; and (2) PathMNIST : 107,180 colon pathology images across 9 tissue types ( 28 ×28 

RGB), split into 89,996/10,004/7,180. These represent fundamentally different imaging modalities (radiology vs. histopathology) and ambiguity structures. 

Baselines. We compare: (1) Naive : softmax thresholding ( πk(x) ≥ 1−α); (2) LAC :label-conditional adaptive CP [9]; (3) RAPS (Size) : λ optimized for mini-mal average set size, without temperature scaling ( T =1 ); (4) RAPS (Temp) :RAPS (Size) with temperature scaling ( T optimized via SGD on Dcal ); and (5) RAPS (Adaptive) : our stratified minimax criterion. 

Metrics. All experiments use α=0 .1 (90% target coverage). We report: (1) Marginal Coverage : global validity check; (2) Average Set Size (APSS) : efficiency metric (lower is better); (3) Singleton Rate : proportion of single-label out-puts; (4) Empty Rate : proportion of empty prediction sets; and (5) Strat. Min. : the worst-case coverage across all populated prediction set size strata, which is the key safety indicator.

## 5 Discussion 

5.1 Safety–Efficiency Trade-off and Bilateral Ambiguity 

Our experiments reveal that standard RAPS converges to near-deterministic behavior by selecting an excessively aggressive λ, dominated by the “easy” ma-jority. For ambiguous samples, coverage collapses to 40–60% (Tables 2–3). The Adaptive Lambda criterion restores stratum-level coverage to ≥90% at a modest cost of ∼9% increase in average set size. 5.2 Theoretical Considerations 

Distribution-free stratum-level coverage guarantees are fundamentally impossi-ble without additional assumptions on the data-generating process [17]; standard CP only provides marginal guarantees over the entire test distribution [23]. Our Adaptive Lambda criterion is therefore a practical safety heuristic that empir-ically minimizes worst-case stratified violations. The consistent improvements across both datasets suggest the minimax strategy is an effective remedy for safety-critical deployments. 

5.3 Clinical Implications and Limitations 

From a regulatory perspective regarding Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) [18], uniformity of performance is paramount. Well-calibrated prediction sets can meaningfully improve human–AI decision-making [25]. The Adaptive Lambda framework aligns with these requirements by ensuring the “90% probability of inclusion” applies to every patient.
